<section class="mb-12">
<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">Why I Recommend This</h2>
<div class="prose prose-lg">
<p>Daub names a pattern I had felt but not articulated: ideas presented as unprecedented in technology often arrive with their intellectual history stripped away. He traces seven keywords—disruption, genius, and failure among them—back to older arguments and asks what disappeared in translation.</p>
<p>His pattern is that novelty claims can hide older regulatory and political questions. Calling a company a “platform” or an activity “ride-sharing” can redirect attention from publisher, taxi, labor, or common-carrier analogies. Those analogies do not settle how any present company should be classified; they restore history to the argument.</p>
</div>
</section>

<!-- THE BOOK -->
<section class="mb-12">
<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">The Book</h2>
<div class="prose prose-lg">
<p>Daub argues that many of Silicon Valley’s “revolutionary ideas” are older motifs in new clothes. He traces seven keywords—dropping out, content, genius, communication, desire, disruption, failure—back to their intellectual histories.</p>
<p>His core argument is that treating every challenge as unprecedented can deprive the public of analytical tools developed for similar problems. Terms such as “platform” and “ride-sharing” can obscure older questions about publishers, taxis, labor classification, and common-carrier obligations. In Daub’s telling, the language functions as a smokescreen.</p>
</div>
</section>
