<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">What Stayed</h2>
<div class="prose prose-lg">
<p>Kingsley begins with the reports of Abaris the Hyperborean arriving in Greece and recognizing Pythagoras. Modern scholarship can dismiss the encounter as legend. He reads its neglected details—the northern origin, the healer and wonder-worker, the arrow—as clues.</p>
<p>That method stays with me even if the reconstruction does not. Before calling a story irrational, learn what its details meant in the world that preserved it.</p>
</div>
</section>

<section class="mb-12">
<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">The Argument</h2>
<div class="prose prose-lg">
<p>Abaris is the hinge. Kingsley compares the Greek accounts with evidence about Central Asian, Mongolian, and Tibetan shamanic traditions, then argues that Abaris helped activate a transmission carried into the Pythagorean tradition.</p>
<p>The book expands that encounter into a claim about Western origins: Greece did not invent its intellectual culture in isolation. A short narrative carries the claim; extensive notes carry the historical case.</p>
</div>
