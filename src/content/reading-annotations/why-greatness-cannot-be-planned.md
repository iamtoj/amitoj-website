<!-- THE BOOK -->
<section class="mb-12">
<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">The Book</h2>
<div class="prose prose-lg">
<p>Stanley and Lehman argue that ambitious objectives actively block discovery. The problem is structural: prerequisites to great achievements rarely resemble the achievement itself. Feathers evolved for temperature regulation long before flight. The transistor emerged from amplification research. Tim Berners-Lee built information management systems. When stepping stones diverge from the goal, objective-driven search gets stuck in local optima that seem like progress but lead nowhere.</p>
<p>Their alternative comes from evolutionary computation: novelty search, an algorithm that rewards behavioral novelty rather than progress toward a specified objective. In the experimental domains they report, novelty search found solutions that objective-driven optimization missed. The book then extends that result to education, science funding, innovation management, and life planning; those extensions are arguments by analogy, not additional computational demonstrations.</p>
</div>
</section>

<section class="mb-12">
<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">Passages That Stayed With Me</h2>
<div class="space-y-6">
<blockquote class="border-l-2 border-sage-400 pl-6">
<p class="text-lg italic mb-2">"In other words (and here is the paradox), the greatest achievements become less likely when they are made objectives. The best way to achieve greatness, the truest path to 'blue sky' discovery or to fulfill boundless ambition, is to have no objective at all."</p>
<p class="text-sm text-ink-muted">The core paradox.</p>
</blockquote>
<blockquote class="border-l-2 border-sage-400 pl-6">
<p class="text-lg italic mb-2">"Contrary to popular belief, great inventors don't peer into the distant future. A false visionary might try to look past the horizon, but a true innovator looks nearby for the next stepping stone. The successful inventor asks where we can get from here rather than how we can get there. It's a subtle yet profound difference."</p>
<p class="text-sm text-ink-muted">Innovation as exploration of the adjacent possible.</p>
</blockquote>
<blockquote class="border-l-2 border-sage-400 pl-6">
<p class="text-lg italic mb-2">"No matter how tempting it is to believe in it, the distant objective cannot guide you to itself—it is the ultimate false compass."</p>
<p class="text-sm text-ink-muted">Deceptive fitness landscapes.</p>
</blockquote>
<blockquote class="border-l-2 border-sage-400 pl-6">
<p class="text-lg italic mb-2">"Evolution is the ultimate treasure hunter, searching for nothing and finding everything."</p>
<p class="text-sm text-ink-muted">The paradigm case.</p>
</blockquote>
<blockquote class="border-l-2 border-sage-400 pl-6">
<p class="text-lg italic mb-2">"Objectives might sometimes provide meaning or direction, but they also limit our freedom and become straitjackets around our desire to explore. After all, when everything we do is measured against its contribution to achieving one objective or another, it robs us of the chance for playful discovery."</p>
<p class="text-sm text-ink-muted">The authors’ extension from experimental search to measurement culture.</p>
</blockquote>
</div>
</section>
