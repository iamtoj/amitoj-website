<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">What Stayed</h2>
<div class="prose prose-lg">
<p>The standard commons problem begins by assuming that shared resources require either private ownership or external command. Ostrom’s cases show that those alternatives do not exhaust the field.</p>
<p>Her design principles make governance inspectable: who can participate, how rules meet local conditions, who monitors use, and how violations and conflicts are handled. They are questions an institution must answer, not a blueprint that guarantees endurance.</p>
</div>
</section>

<section class="mb-12">
<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">The Argument</h2>
<div class="prose prose-lg">
<p>Ostrom compares communities governing alpine meadows, forests, irrigation systems, fishing grounds, and groundwater basins. She asks how people in interdependent situations organize themselves despite incentives to free-ride or shirk.</p>
<p>From those cases she derives eight recurring design features, including defined boundaries, collective choice, monitoring, graduated sanctions, accessible conflict resolution, and nested governance. The cases establish the possibility of self-governance under particular conditions without reducing institutional design to one universal form.</p>
</div>
