<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">What Stayed</h2>
<div class="prose prose-lg">
<p>Calling alchemy failed chemistry assumes that chemistry was what alchemy was trying to become. Eliade rejects that foundation. For the metallurgical traditions he describes, ore was alive, fire accelerated a natural maturation, and transformation involved the worker as well as the material.</p>
<p>The smith therefore does not appear as a primitive chemist with bad explanations. He occupies a different relation to matter—one in which resistance, craft, and sacred participation belong to the operation itself.</p>
</div>
</section>

<section class="mb-12">
<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">The Argument</h2>
<div class="prose prose-lg">
<p>Eliade traces metallurgical and alchemical traditions across Chinese, Indian, and Western contexts. He reconstructs a view of ores as gestating within the earth and of the smith as participating in their maturation.</p>
<p>Alchemy joins two transformations: work upon metals and work upon the person performing it. Chemistry begins, in Eliade’s account, when substances lose those sacred attributes and material transformation becomes separable from transformation of the worker.</p>
</div>
