<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">Why I Recommend This</h2>
<div class="prose prose-lg">
<p>Scott gave me a vocabulary for a recurring design risk: <em>mētis</em>, the practical knowledge a formal schema may not capture. An org chart can name reporting lines without recording who knows what, who trusts whom, or which exceptions keep work moving. The book asks what a legibility project destroys when it mistakes the diagram for the system.</p>
<p>Scott traces this risk across efforts to make societies legible—standard surnames, cadastral maps, scientific forestry, planned cities. In his account, simplification can enable control while eliminating local knowledge. Combined with authoritarian power and a weak civil society, he argues, the result can be catastrophic. I retain one question: what practical knowledge does the plan fail to represent?</p>
</div>
</section>

<section class="mb-12">
<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">The Book</h2>
<div class="prose prose-lg">
<p>Scott’s claim is that states do not merely describe the world; they can reshape it to match their descriptions. A cadastral map that designates property owners creates a new property regime. Standard surnames reorganize kinship and identity. Scientific forestry replaces diverse ecosystems with monocultural plantations. This process of making society "legible"—visible, countable, manageable from a central vantage point—enables state projects but also blinds the state to crucial local knowledge.</p>
<p>Scott identifies four conditions that, when combined, produce catastrophic outcomes: administrative ordering of nature and society, high-modernist ideology (the "muscle-bound" confidence in scientific progress and rational design), an authoritarian state willing to use coercion, and a prostrate civil society unable to resist. Remove any element and disaster can be avoided. Democratic accountability and robust civil society constrain even convinced high modernists. The framework explains why some societies suffer catastrophic planning failures while others muddle through.</p>
</div>
</section>

<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">Passages That Stayed With Me</h2>
<div class="space-y-6">
<blockquote class="border-l-2 border-sage-400 pl-6">
<p class="text-lg italic mb-2">"The premodern state was, in many crucial respects, partially blind; it knew precious little about its subjects, their wealth, their landholdings and yields, their location, their very identity."</p>
<p class="text-sm text-ink-muted">In Scott’s account, legibility solves a real problem and creates another.</p>
</blockquote>
<blockquote class="border-l-2 border-sage-400 pl-6">
<p class="text-lg italic mb-2">"Designed or planned social order is necessarily schematic; it always ignores essential features of any real, functioning social order."</p>
<p class="text-sm text-ink-muted">Scott’s warning: every schema leaves part of a functioning order out.</p>
</blockquote>
<blockquote class="border-l-2 border-sage-400 pl-6">
<p class="text-lg italic mb-2">"Mētis represents a wide array of practical skills and acquired intelligence in responding to a constantly changing natural and human environment."</p>
<p class="text-sm text-ink-muted">The Greek term for what you know but can't quite explain.</p>
</blockquote>
<blockquote class="border-l-2 border-sage-400 pl-6">
<p class="text-lg italic mb-2">"Centrally managed social plans misfire when they impose schematic visions that do violence to complex interdependencies that are not—and cannot—be fully understood."</p>
<p class="text-sm text-ink-muted">Scott treats the failure as structural, not accidental.</p>
</blockquote>
<blockquote class="border-l-2 border-sage-400 pl-6">
<p class="text-lg italic mb-2">"The aspiration to such uniformity and order alerts us to the fact that modern statecraft is largely a project of internal colonization, often glossed as a 'civilizing mission.'"</p>
<p class="text-sm text-ink-muted">Scott’s indictment of high-modernist statecraft.</p>
</blockquote>
