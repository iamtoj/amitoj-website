<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">What Stayed</h2>
<div class="prose prose-lg">
<p>To say that a technology changes everything assumes that the relevant comparison has already been chosen. The Durants begin at a scale large enough to make that assumption visible: five thousand years of recurring drives, institutions, conflicts, and claims of novelty.</p>
<p>I value the scale because it disciplines the novelty claim. It does not make the printing press, industrialization, nuclear weapons, and AI equivalent. It asks which layer changed, which layer persisted, and what evidence would distinguish this transformation from its predecessors.</p>
</div>

<section class="mb-12">
<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">The Argument</h2>
<div class="prose prose-lg">
<p>After writing their eleven-volume history of civilization, Will and Ariel Durant compressed the patterns they believed survived comparison. Their central distinction is between a relatively persistent human nature and an accumulated cultural inheritance that each generation must learn again.</p>
<p>The book treats progress as real but precarious. Technology changes constraints, institutions transmit accumulated learning, and opposing systems can alter one another through sustained pressure.</p>
</div>
