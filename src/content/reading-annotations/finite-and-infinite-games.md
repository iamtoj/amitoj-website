<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">What Stayed</h2>
<div class="prose prose-lg">
<p>A contract can settle its stated terms and still damage the relationship needed to carry them. Carse’s distinction makes that tension visible: one side can win the stated game while reducing the possibility of future play.</p>
<p>The distinction is useful as a question, not a decision rule. Is a strategy session mistaking conclusion for progress? Is a collaboration becoming a competition? Is learning being reduced to credentials?</p>
</div>
</section>

<section class="mb-12">
<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">The Argument</h2>
<div class="prose prose-lg">
<p>Finite games are played to win. They have boundaries, rules, and a conclusion. Infinite games are played to continue; their rules can change when continuation is threatened.</p>
<p>Carse develops the distinction through 101 numbered aphorisms about power, education, society, nature, and war. His strongest claim is not that finite games disappear. It is that finite play becomes destructive when it colonizes a practice whose value depends on remaining open.</p>
</div>
