<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">What Stayed</h2>
<div class="prose prose-lg">
<p>Jaynes reads the <em>Iliad</em> as containing less reflective deliberation than modern readers expect. He treats commands voiced by gods as evidence of an earlier mentality organized around auditory authority.</p>
<p>The literary observation can motivate the theory without settling it. What I retain for organizations is also an analogy: a group may seek authorization outside itself, but that pattern does not show that the group preserves a bicameral structure.</p>
</div>
</section>

<section class="mb-12">
<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">The Argument</h2>
<div class="prose prose-lg">
<p>Jaynes proposes that reflective consciousness emerged after the breakdown of an earlier bicameral mentality. In that account, one hemisphere generated commands experienced as divine voices while the other obeyed.</p>
<p>Social upheaval, writing, and increasing complexity weakened that organization. Consciousness then developed as a metaphorical mind-space containing an “analog I.” Jaynes reads hallucination, hypnosis, possession, and poetic inspiration as possible remnants. Each step depends on a contested historical and neurological reconstruction.</p>
</div>
