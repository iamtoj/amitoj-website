<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">What Stayed</h2>
<div class="prose prose-lg">
<p>Walking out of a week-long silent retreat, the parking lot sounded violent. The quiet had recalibrated my senses enough for re-entry to make the change visible.</p>
<p>Abram gives that experience a history: perception is reciprocal, while alphabetic literacy redirected participation from landscape toward text. The scene establishes perceptual recalibration rather than that history. The question I keep is what forms of attention literacy trains and what it leaves less practiced.</p>
</div>
</section>

<section class="mb-12">
<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">The Argument</h2>
<div class="prose prose-lg">
<p>Abram begins from Merleau-Ponty’s account of embodied perception as reciprocity between seer and seen. He extends it into an argument about oral cultures, animate landscapes, and alphabetic writing.</p>
<p>On his account, the Greek alphabet severed signs from visible features of the world and concentrated attention on human speech. The argument joins phenomenology to a broad cultural history. Its grouping of distinct Indigenous traditions requires more caution than the book consistently supplies.</p>
</div>
