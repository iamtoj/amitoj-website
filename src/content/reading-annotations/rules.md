<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">What Stayed</h2>
<div class="prose prose-lg">
<p>The demand for a complete rule assumes that application can be specified before the case arrives. Daston shows why that starting point fails. A universal and a particular rarely align without interpretation.</p>
<p>Thick rules keep examples, caveats, and discretion visible. Thin rules try to remove them. The thin rule appears more complete only because it pushes the work of judgment out of view.</p>
</div>
</section>

<section class="mb-12">
<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">The Argument</h2>
<div class="prose prose-lg">
<p>Daston traces three durable forms of rules: algorithms to execute, laws to obey, and models to imitate. The model dominated for much of Western history because it made interpretation part of competent rule-following.</p>
<p>The modern aspiration to context-free rules elevated the algorithm. Daston’s history shows what that aspiration presupposes: an ordered world in which the relevant cases and adjustments have already been settled.</p>
</div>
