<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">What Stayed</h2>
<div class="prose prose-lg">
<p>A book about meaning in the camps becomes dangerous when meaning is allowed to explain who survived. Frankl offers testimony and a therapeutic proposition, not a controlled comparison of survival.</p>
<p>What I retain is more limited and more demanding: under some unavoidable conditions, meaning can alter a person’s relation to suffering. It neither justifies the suffering nor makes that response universally available.</p>
</div>
</section>

<section class="mb-12">
<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">The Argument</h2>
<div class="prose prose-lg">
<p>Frankl describes his imprisonment and presents logotherapy, the therapeutic approach he had begun developing before the war. He identifies three routes to meaning: creation, encounter, and the stance taken toward unavoidable suffering.</p>
<p>The final route is the riskiest. It names a response Frankl believed could remain available without making suffering necessary, deserved, or clinically useful.</p>
</div>
