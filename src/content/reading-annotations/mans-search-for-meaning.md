<section class="mb-12">
<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">Why I Recommend This</h2>
<div class="prose prose-lg">
<p>Frankl wrote about meaning from inside the camps and later through logotherapy. I recommend the book for the question it keeps open: what forms of responsibility remain when circumstances cannot be chosen? His testimony does not establish a universal rule about who endures, and it should not be read as clinical guidance or an explanation of survival.</p>
<p>The narrower claim I take from it is that meaning can alter how some people relate to unavoidable difficulty. It does not make suffering necessary, deserved, or automatically bearable.</p>
</div>
</section>

<!-- THE BOOK -->
<section class="mb-12">
<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">The Book</h2>
<div class="prose prose-lg">
<p>Frankl argues that the search for meaning is a primary human drive. He developed logotherapy before the war and later wrote about his imprisonment in concentration camps. The memoir is testimony and interpretation, not a controlled comparison of who survived or why.</p>
<p>The second half presents logotherapy. Frankl describes three pathways to meaning: what a person creates, whom or what a person encounters, and the attitude taken toward unavoidable suffering. I read the final pathway as his therapeutic proposition about a remaining response, not proof that everyone can choose an attitude under every condition.</p>
</div>
</section>

<!-- PASSAGES -->
<section class="mb-12">
<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">Passages That Stayed With Me</h2>
<div class="space-y-6">
<blockquote class="border-l-2 border-sage-400 pl-6">
<p class="text-lg italic mb-2">"Everything can be taken from a man but one thing: the last of the human freedoms—to choose one's attitude in any given set of circumstances, to choose one's own way."</p>
<p class="text-sm text-ink-muted">Frankl’s claim about a remaining freedom under coercion; his testimony does not make the claim universal.</p>
</blockquote>
<blockquote class="border-l-2 border-sage-400 pl-6">
<p class="text-lg italic mb-2">"Ultimately, man should not ask what the meaning of his life is, but rather must recognize that it is he who is asked. In a word, each man is questioned by life; and he can only answer to life by answering for his own life; to life he can only respond by being responsible."</p>
<p class="text-sm text-ink-muted">Frankl reverses the direction. Life is the questioner.</p>
</blockquote>
<blockquote class="border-l-2 border-sage-400 pl-6">
<p class="text-lg italic mb-2">"In some way, suffering ceases to be suffering at the moment it finds a meaning, such as the meaning of a sacrifice."</p>
<p class="text-sm text-ink-muted">Frankl’s account of how meaning can alter an experience of unavoidable suffering.</p>
</blockquote>
<blockquote class="border-l-2 border-sage-400 pl-6">
<p class="text-lg italic mb-2">"Being human always points, and is directed, to something or someone, other than oneself—be it a meaning to fulfill or another human being to encounter. The more one forgets himself—by giving himself to a cause to serve or another person to love—the more human he is and the more he actualizes himself."</p>
<p class="text-sm text-ink-muted">Self-actualization as byproduct. The direct pursuit defeats itself.</p>
</blockquote>
<blockquote class="border-l-2 border-sage-400 pl-6">
<p class="text-lg italic mb-2">"Ever more people today have the means to live, but no meaning to live for."</p>
<p class="text-sm text-ink-muted">A diagnosis Frankl made in his own time, not a measured claim about ours.</p>
</blockquote>
</div>
</section>
