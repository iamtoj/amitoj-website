<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">What Stayed</h2>
<div class="prose prose-lg">
<p>Traditional theism and a wholly disenchanted atheism are often presented as the available choices. Kronman refuses that starting point. He asks whether the world itself can bear the religious weight once assigned to a supernatural God.</p>
<p>His answer is <em>Deus sive Natura</em>: God or Nature. The move interests me because it lets contemplative practice carry theological significance without requiring supernatural belief.</p>
</div>
</section>

<section class="mb-12">
<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">The Argument</h2>
<div class="prose prose-lg">
<p>Kronman identifies God with “the inexhaustible and everlasting presence of the world itself.” He develops that position through Aristotle, Spinoza, Whitman, and Nietzsche, then calls the resulting synthesis born-again paganism.</p>
<p>The book’s ambition is not to restore institutional faith. It is to recover reverence, mortality, and spiritual formation inside a world compatible with modern reason.</p>
</div>
</section>
