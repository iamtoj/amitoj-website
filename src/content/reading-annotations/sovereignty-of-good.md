<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">Why I Recommend This</h2>
<div class="prose prose-lg">
<p>Murdoch’s essays begin from a limit in choice-centered ethics: the moment of decision depends on what a person has already learned to notice. Case studies can make trade-offs visible; they cannot ensure that the relevant features of a new situation will appear. That gap is why I return to this book.</p>
<p>Murdoch says moral life is mostly about the slow, patient work of attention that determines what you can see when a choice arrives. "I can only choose within the world I can see." If vision is clouded by fantasy, ego, or inattention, the right option may never even appear. The work isn't in choosing. It's in learning to see clearly.</p>
</div>
</section>

<!-- THE BOOK -->
<section class="mb-12">
<h2 class="text-sm text-ink-muted uppercase tracking-wider mb-4">The Book</h2>
<div class="prose prose-lg">
<p>Murdoch's radical claim: moral life is about vision, not choice. Against the prevailing philosophy of her time, which located morality in public action and discrete moments of decision, she argues that "I can only choose within the world I can see." Our perception is shaped by accumulated practices of attention long before we face any choice.</p>
<p>The Good, for Murdoch, is not projection or construction but transcendent reality that functions like Plato's sun: not directly visible, but the source of light by which all else is seen. The path toward it requires "unselfing," the disciplined reduction of ego's distortions.</p>
</div>
</section>

<p class="text-sm text-ink-muted">Murdoch's claim: ontological, not sentimental. Love as the capacity to perceive another person's reality.</p>
