I spent weeks convinced I had solved a theoretical problem: whether firms change because they discover new information or because their preferences shift. The same observed change could carry either explanation.

I had notes, citations, and cases from the literature that seemed to support the distinction. Then I stopped writing and asked: what was the strongest argument against it?

The counterarguments I generated followed concerns already inside the framework. Building the case had also shaped the objections available to me. A colleague could supply a premise or case I had not placed there, but useful disagreement required someone knowledgeable and available while the question was still live. I began testing whether model calls could stage that pressure before a colleague entered.

---

The idea came from my Digital Twin setup—the system I described in The Dot Collector. I had already used a model to reflect patterns in my writing. This time I wanted something less agreeable: three model calls occupying different positions around the same argument.

One defended the framework from the assumptions already in my notes. A second had one job: find the strongest objection and say what evidence would break the claim. A third tried to locate the disagreement—whether the first two were contesting the same thing, and what formulation could preserve what each had shown.

---

The first time I ran the structure on my firm-evolution framework, the critic went after the distinction itself. Discovery and preference change both end with the firm doing something different. What observation would tell them apart?

I felt the jolt of finding fuzziness where I had expected precision. The two mechanisms were conceptually distinct, but I had no empirical test for assigning a particular change to one rather than the other.

The advocate proposed timing relative to new information and behavioral consistency before and after the change. Neither proposal yet separated the mechanisms in the evidence.

The synthesizer relocated the distinction. Discovery and preference change could remain separate explanations even when the available evidence did not separate them for prediction. In a particular case, telling them apart might require access to the firm’s internal deliberations.

The framework survived as an explanatory distinction, not a forecasting instrument. The empirical test it needed was still missing.

---

A staged critic had forced an objection into view that my own counterarguments had missed. Its value came from the structure: defense, objection, and synthesis made the disagreement explicit enough for me to answer.

The structure also had a boundary. All three calls began from roles I assigned and material I supplied, so their disagreement could reproduce the same omissions. A colleague can bring evidence or experience I did not put in the prompt. That disagreement does not begin from my staging.

I used the same arrangement on later drafts. Sometimes the framework survived; sometimes its boundaries changed. The local test was whether the staged objection exposed a claim I could now answer or test.

The weeks of solo work built the framework. The staged critic found where it stopped. I kept the procedure for that reason, without mistaking the role-play for a colleague.
