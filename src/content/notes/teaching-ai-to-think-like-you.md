I was standing in our kitchen watching my mom season a pot of dal. She had cooked the same dishes for our family my whole life, with movements and results I could never replicate. When I asked how much salt to add, she looked at me as though I had asked how to breathe. “You taste it,” she said. “Then you know.”

I did not know; that was why I had asked. Something in her hands had not made it into her words, and watching could not transfer it directly into mine.

---

My Digital Twin keeps a record of my notes and writing. It retrieves across that record and proposes connections I did not state in advance.

During one writing review, it returned a sentence that stopped me mid-scroll. I had gestured at an idea without grounding it, and the feedback named the weakness: “This reads like assertion rather than argument.” It then pointed to a pattern in my earlier work: at this level, I usually bring in an example or a citation.

The pattern was recognizably mine: I often support claims that way, but I had never written the preference down as a rule. The system had proposed a regularity from earlier writing and feedback. Its test was whether it would predict another choice.

---

Michael Polanyi compressed the problem into a sentence—“We know more than we can tell”—and used riding a bicycle as one example. A description of balance can be accurate without transferring the ability to balance.

Documentation can preserve what someone can articulate. It could record my mother’s instruction—taste it, then you know—but it could not put the knowing in my hand.

The writing example differs at the crucial point. The model did not have to possess my judgment to describe a regularity in my choices. My mother did not give me a quantity for the salt; I had not stated the writing preference as a rule. In both cases, description remained separate from the judgment required to make the next choice.

---

The system retained more of my recorded history than I could hold in working memory. Before revealing a choice, I sometimes asked it to predict from earlier examples. Some predictions matched; others did not. Each result put an unstated pattern against another choice.

That returns me to the salt. Repeated observations of my mother’s hand might reveal a regularity in the gesture. A regularity could predict the next movement without putting the knowledge of seasoning in my hand.

---

What the model gave me was a pattern I could inspect. The next choice supplied its test: if the pattern could not travel beyond the examples that produced it, it had described the reach for salt without telling me when enough was enough.
