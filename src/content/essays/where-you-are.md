# Where You Are

I am unhappy.

Stay with that for a moment. Not why, not since when, not compared to what. Just the fact of it, sitting in the chest, untheorized. There is no path forward from this sentence and no resolution inside it. It is a condition.

Now: I am unhappy because I am hungry.

The relief is immediate. The ground firms up beneath you. You know what to do — find food. But notice what happened in the space between those two sentences. "Unhappy" contained multitudes: loneliness, exhaustion, grief, boredom, meaninglessness, and yes, hunger. "Unhappy because hungry" contains one. The causal theory didn't illuminate the problem. It replaced it.

---

A friend says "I don't know what to do with my life." I find that most of us — myself included — reach for a cause almost immediately. Have you thought about what you're good at? What did you enjoy before the burnout? The response feels helpful, and it is helpful, in the narrow sense that it provides a next step. But something has happened in the giving of that help: an existential problem has become a career-matching exercise. The original statement — I don't know what to do with my life — is gone. Replaced by a more tractable version.

I'm not confident I know how to do this differently. The pull toward theorizing is a deeply trained reflex. We reach for causes the way we reach for handrails — automatically, and for good reason. Causes give us traction. They make problems actionable. What I find striking is that the reflex fires before we've stayed with what's actually in front of us. The cause arrives and the relief is real, and in the relief we lose contact with the problem's original shape.

---

This happens at every scale. A company says: people are lonely. That's a problem — felt, real, dimensionally rich. Then someone in the room says "because they don't have enough ways to connect," and now the problem has a direction. Build a platform. Increase connection volume. Measure engagement. Ship features.

But what if loneliness has nothing to do with connection volume? What if it's about the quality of attention in the connections you already have — or the absence of being known by someone who has no use for you? What if it's something no one in the room has language for yet, something that would only become visible by staying with "people are lonely" long enough for its actual shape to emerge?

The two commitments here are different in kind, and the difference matters. Committing to "people are lonely" is evaluative: it says *this matters*, and it holds open everything about what to do. Committing to "because they lack connection" is instrumental: it says *this will work*, and it closes most of the search space in the same breath. One is a statement about value. The other is a bet on causation. The bet feels like the value statement — having a direction feels like understanding — and that is where the substitution becomes invisible.

---

I want to be careful not to dismiss what theorizing accomplishes. The tools we've inherited for engaging with problems — given an end, find the optimal path; given a symptom, identify the cause; given a question, narrow toward an answer — are genuinely powerful. When the end is settled, convergence works. If you know you're hungry, these tools will find you food faster than anything else. The achievement is real and I don't think acknowledging its limits diminishes it.

But what happens when settling the end is itself the task? "I am unhappy" doesn't behave like a question waiting for an answer. It's a condition. Staying with it — feeling its dimensionality without reaching for a cause — would mean widening into the problem's actual shape rather than narrowing toward a resolution. And we have almost no infrastructure for this. Education, medicine, management, policy: the sequence is identify the problem, find the cause, fix it. The sequence is so deeply internalized that staying with a problem — letting it remain open, resisting the reflex — registers as stalling.

What strikes me is how structural this is. The apparatus of rational problem-solving has a feature that goes mostly unnoticed: it requires an endpoint in order to operate. Convergence needs somewhere to converge toward. When no end has been settled, the machinery manufactures one — by theorizing the problem into a problem-solution pair. The theory provides the endpoint that the convergence machinery requires. And the replacement is invisible because the relief of having a direction feels identical to the experience of understanding.

---

Herbert Simon gave the name "bounded rationality" to the constraint on optimizing toward a given end: you can't compute the best path, so you satisfice. But there is a prior constraint his framework takes as given — the limit on articulating the end itself.

Call this bounded articulation. The two limits look parallel, but they're structurally inverse. Bounded rationality is computational: more processing power helps, better algorithms help, the constraint yields to ingenuity. Bounded articulation does not yield this way. You can't converge your way to an end worth converging toward. Formalizing what matters commits the very error the concept identifies: it forces something essentially divergent through convergence machinery. The theory of the problem replaces the problem.

What can stay with a problem without converting it? I think the answer is closer to awareness than to analysis — the capacity to remain in contact with a condition before the reflex to explain, diagnose, and fix takes over. I'm not confident I can specify this further without committing the same error. Awareness, if it means anything here, means the discipline of not leaving. The discomfort is the information. The urge to resolve it is the thing that destroys what the discomfort has to teach.

I am unhappy. A sentence that contains everything and specifies nothing. The right direction presupposes where you are. And where you are is the one thing the machinery of convergence cannot tell you.
